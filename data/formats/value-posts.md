# value post format

## what makes these posts work

Every value post opens with a specific, slightly uncomfortable claim backed by a number, so the reader stops because something they believe is being challenged or something they want is being promised. He keeps them reading with constant open loops ("here's why:", "here's the breakdown:") placed right before every payoff, and with one-line paragraphs that make each line feel like a step down a staircase. They save because the middle of the post is a reusable tool: a ranked list, a formula, a bad/good comparison, or a step-by-step they can apply later. They trust him because almost every claim is tied to his own result or a client result with odd, precise numbers, and because he shows the mechanism behind each point instead of just stating it. He also names the reader's current behavior before correcting it, so the reader feels seen first and taught second.

## hooks

Numbers in the first line are always specific and usually odd or unrounded (1.1M, 1,833, 26, $10.5k, 72k). He pairs the number with a constraint or a timeframe so the result reads as surprising rather than merely large. The first line is almost never a question and never starts with "how to". The second and third lines either stack proof under the claim or open the loop that the rest of the post closes.

**1. Result plus constraint, then the system tease**
What it does: proves authority in line one and promises the method.
Template:
```
i [achieved specific result] [despite constraint / within timeframe]

[optional: 2 to 4 stacked lines of secondary numbers]

here's the [named system / exact process] i used:
```
When to use: whenever there is a real result to anchor the post. This is his default opener for breakdowns and case studies.

**2. The contrarian attack**
What it does: picks a fight with a popular belief, then shows proof in line two so the fight looks earned.
Template:
```
the "[popular advice or trend]" [trend / advice] is [strong dismissal]

i've [specific result] [using the thing everyone says doesn't work]

here's the nuance you're missing:
```
When to use: when the audience is hearing advice you disagree with and you can back your side with your own numbers.

**3. The surprising case from outside the field**
What it does: borrows attention from a famous or unexpected example, then promises a lesson the reader can apply.
Template:
```
[famous person / company] [did surprising thing / has surprising trait]

and that's a [field] masterclass

here's why:
```
When to use: when one principle can be shown more vividly through an outside example than through your own work.

**4. The "only N" hard limit**
What it does: compresses a whole field into a small, closed number, which feels authoritative and is easy to save.
Template:
```
there are ONLY [small number] [things] that [desired outcome]:
```
or
```
[result] because [i did X]

and there are only [number] main ways of doing so:
```
When to use: short list posts where each item fits on one line.

**5. The pattern discovery**
What it does: positions the writer as someone who looked at more examples than anyone else, then reveals a pattern.
Template:
```
i've [analyzed / reviewed / tested] [large specific number] [things]

and there's a pattern that explains why most [readers] [fail at outcome]
```
When to use: diagnostic posts that correct a common setup or habit.

**6. The loss and recovery**
What it does: opens on a problem the reader is also feeling, then shows the writer already solved it.
Template:
```
[external change] killed my [results]

but instead of [common reaction]
i [tested / changed something]

now [new specific result]

here's what [died / changed / works now]:
```
When to use: right after a change in the industry, tools, or market that is hurting everyone.

**7. The side-by-side data hook**
What it does: puts two real data sets in front of the reader so they draw the conclusion themselves.
Template:
```
[claim about which option wins]

let's compare [two real examples] so you get it:

[option A label]:
[metric]
[metric]
[metric]

[option B label]:
[metric]
[metric]
[metric]

see?
```
When to use: when you have two real results that contradict what people expect.

**8. The observed habit**
What it does: describes a mistake as a scene the reader has watched happen.
Template:
```
i see this all the time

people hear [authority] say "[advice]"

then [do the shallow version of it]
```
When to use: mistake and fix posts.

## developing a point

Every point in his longer posts follows the same order, and he rarely skips a step.

1. **Claim.** One line, often with one word in caps. It states a rule, not a topic.
2. **Mechanism.** Opens with "because" or "here's why". Explains what is actually happening underneath, in 2 to 6 short lines. He goes one level deeper than the reader expects, usually to the motive of whoever controls the outcome (the platform, the buyer, the competitor).
3. **Consequence.** "which means", "so", or an equation chain like `more [A] = more [B]`. This connects the mechanism back to money, time, or results.
4. **Concrete example.** Either a bad/good pair, a real number from his work, or an imagined reaction from the reader's buyer written as a quote or as `*response*: [reaction]`. The reaction lines carry a lot of persuasion: they let the reader feel the difference instead of being told it.
5. **Practical implication.** "this is why [practice] works". Ends the point with something to do.

Depth: in a long post each point runs 6 to 15 lines. In a short post the same chain is squeezed into a claim plus one mechanism line plus one example. What he never skips is the mechanism and the example. A point with no "because" and no specific illustration does not appear in his value posts.

He reuses three devices inside points:
- **The contrast couplet:** two lines with the same structure and one word swapped. For example: "one is expected / other is impressive."
- **The same input, different framing check:** "same [raw material] / different [framing] / different [outcome]".
- **The worked example:** he shows the inputs, the steps, and the finished output, so the reader can copy the process.

## structure and typography

- **Single-line paragraphs** with a blank line between almost every line. Two lines are joined without a gap only when they form a couplet or a short list.
- **All lowercase** for body text. Caps are reserved for section labels, step labels, and one or two emphasized words per section.
- **Section labels in caps ending in a colon:** `WHY IT MATTERS:`, `THE REAL ISSUE WITH "[X]":`, `SO HOW DO YOU APPLY THIS?`, `THE LESSON:`. In long posts a label appears every 8 to 20 lines.
- **Numbered points** take four forms depending on the post: `1.` for formulas, `#1 - [ITEM]` for rankings, `STEP 1:` for processes, `[NOUN] 1:` (PATTERN, ELEMENT, LAYER, PHASE) for systems. Each numbered label is followed by a one-line explanation before anything else.
- **Hyphen lists** for deliverables, tactics, and sub-items, usually 3 to 6 items.
- **Unhyphenated stacked lines** for rhythm: three short lines in a row with no markers, often building in length or intensity.
- **Parentheticals on their own line** for asides, caveats, and proof: `(in order of importance)`, `([constraint]: [type])`.
- **Quoted examples** in double quotes on their own lines, almost always in bad then good order.
- **Length bands:** short posts run 40 to 120 words, mid posts 150 to 350, longforms 700 to 2,000+. Value posts with a framework are almost always longforms; the length is part of the promise.
- **Sign-off** on longforms: a hyphen and the first name on its own line.

## transitions and closers

**Transitions** are short lines ending in a colon that open a loop right before the payoff. He uses them at almost every section break:
- `here's why:`
- `here's the [breakdown / play / framework] no one talks about:`
- `let me explain:`
- `for example:`
- `think about it:`
- `but here's the thing:`
- `here's where most people go wrong:`
- `here's the controversial part:` (used near the end to add one admission that strengthens the argument)
- `this EXACT principle applies to [field]:` (for analogy posts)
- `that's when [turning point]:` (for case studies)
- `see?` (after showing data)

**Closers.** He ends with one of these, sometimes two stacked:

1. **Stop/start pairs**
```
stop [old habit]
start [new habit]
```
2. **The ultimate test**
```
if [reader / buyer thinks X] = [bad version]
if [reader / buyer thinks Y] = [good version]

aim for the second
```
3. **The lesson label**
```
lesson:

[one-line principle]
```
4. **The binary question**
```
so the question is simple:
do you want [A] or [B]?
```
5. **The two-group outcome**
```
most people will [ignore this] because [reason]

the ones who [implement it] get [specific outcome]
```
6. **The closing couplet or aphorism**
```
[A] gets [lesser outcome]
[B] gets [real outcome]
```
7. **The save prompt:** `bookmark this` or `bookmark this and implement`.
8. **The keyword CTA**
```
dm me "[KEYWORD]" and i'll [specific deliverable tied to the post]
```
or, for case studies:
```
IF YOU WANT THIS:
i can [promise with number] (and if [condition] you get [guarantee])
dm me "[KEYWORD]"
```
9. **The calm-down note** (for update posts): `final note:` then one reassurance and one instruction to adapt.

## what to avoid

- **Profanity, slurs, and shock jokes about groups of people.** His audience tolerates them; business owners hiring a website builder will not, and they will not survive a screenshot.
- **"Manipulation", "dark patterns", "violence" framing.** He uses it as an edge to attract other marketers. Keep the structure (forced awareness, cost of inaction, bad/good contrast) but drop the villain branding.
- **Manufactured scarcity and inflated or unverifiable numbers.** Only use numbers Ben can back with a real project. Odd specific numbers only build trust if they are true.
- **Insulting the reader** ("bozo", "are you dumb?"). Challenge the belief, not the person.
- **Age and lifestyle flexes and personal shock stories.** They work because his audience is young and aspirational.
- **Insider jargon of his niche** (funnel stage acronyms, platform slang). Use the words Ben's clients use.
- **Repeating "no one talks about" and "masterclass" in every post.** Use each opener device once per post at most.
- **Inconsistent counts** (promising 7 layers and listing 8). Check that the number in the hook matches the list.
- **Attacking named individuals.** He gets reach from it; for Ben it creates risk with no upside.
- **Overlong moral sections.** When he lectures, the post loses the step-by-step tool that makes it saveable. Every section should either teach or prove.
