# self made web designer: philosophy

## core principles

**Make the first screen answer three questions in seconds**
Claim: the top section of a homepage has to say who the business is, what it offers, and what to do next, fast enough that the visitor decides to stay.
Reasoning: he says you have "about three seconds to convince someone that your website was actually worth the click" (his words), and by his own estimate about 85% of visitors care about nothing below the top section. Another of his claims is that only about 20% of people scroll past the first section at all. A confusing first screen makes the brain check out, "not because your design was bad. But because their brain didn't know what to do with it" (his words).
Example: his photographer critique. Many photographer homepages open with a cute statement, and "nowhere on their homepage can you find, okay, where they're located and what kind of photography they do."
(appears in 12 videos)

**Decide what gets to be loud, and make everything else quiet**
Claim: every section needs one element that is clearly most important, a second tier that doesn't compete with it, and small details for people who want them.
Reasoning: people don't follow a fixed F-shaped reading path. They look wherever the design pulls them, so size, contrast, color, spacing, and alignment have to set the order. "If everything on your homepage is important, then nothing is important." "Designing is mostly deciding what gets to be loud and then forcing everything else to shut up." (both his)
Example: the failure he names is a big headline, a big subhead, and a button that is indistinguishable from either.
(appears in 11 videos)

**A prettier site can sell less**
Claim: looking better and converting better are different goals, and chasing the first can hurt the second.
Reasoning: he redesigned a client site that was "clean, it was modern, it was chef's kiss" (his words) and far better looking than the old one. After launch, sales plummeted because he had ignored the user journey. That launch shaped how he has worked since. "If your design doesn't consider conversion, it's actually useless to your clients." (his)
Example: the opposite case comes from Tom Ross, a guest on his show. Clients of Tom's already earning $10,000 to $20,000 a month saw revenue rise 50 to 100 percent "like overnight" after a conversion-focused redesign.
(appears in 10 videos)

**Do less, but do it well**
Claim: sites that feel expensive come from restraint: generous white space, few colors, few typefaces, and few decorative elements.
Reasoning: when everything competes for attention, nothing wins, and the extra elements pull attention away from the words. White space gives visitors "a mental breather" (his words) and makes a page look easy to understand without work. "Expensive looking websites aren't about doing more. They're about doing less, but better." (his)
Example: Apple's sites use one typeface for all text, a palette of black, gray, white, and one blue accent, and lots of space. His warning against the opposite: "eight colors in your color palette, 10 different design elements, and something new to look at every 100 pixels."
(appears in 9 videos)

**People scan, they don't read**
Claim: pages have to work for someone glancing, so text is broken into chunks, lists, and short lines.
Reasoning: he cites a Nielsen study finding "users check out the second they see a big block of copy." Working memory holds about three or four things, so a long run of benefits gets forgotten before the visitor reaches the bottom of the page. "People don't read websites. They scan them." (his)
Example: a visitor scrolling past free shipping, free returns, a warranty, and a loyalty program "has already dropped half of the reasons to buy" (his words) before the page ends. Grouping those benefits into a few clusters fixes it.
(appears in 9 videos)

**If they can't read it, it doesn't matter how pretty it is**
Claim: text contrast and legibility come before style.
Reasoning: low-contrast text, small text, and decorative body fonts all make the visitor work harder, and only a highly motivated visitor will do that work. People with visual impairments get shut out entirely.
Example: Sarah Doody, a UX guest, sees portfolios set in "a font that's 10 point that's some light gray." His own example is small white text laid over floating clouds, which he calls a cool layout that is still hard to read.
(appears in 8 videos)

**The main button has to be the most visible thing on the page**
Claim: calls to action need a solid fill, high contrast, a color reserved for action, and a label that says exactly what happens on click.
Reasoning: clicking a button is the main thing the site wants visitors to do. He says it has "been proven over and over again that ghost buttons get ignored" (his words), meaning outline-only buttons. A vague label makes people unsure what the click does, so fewer people click.
Example: he replaced "Learn more" with "Sign up for my newsletter." He prefers "See pricing" or "Download the guide" over "Let's do this" or "Heck yes."
(appears in 8 videos)

**Sell the outcome, not the features**
Claim: copy should speak to what the buyer is really trying to get, not list what the seller offers or how it's built.
Reasoning: every purchase has a deeper motive than the thing bought. "No one buys a hammer just because they love hammers" (his). They want the hung picture, and behind that the memories it holds. "I'm not shopping for a tech stack. I'm shopping for a result." (his)
Example: Apple never sold iTunes on its library size or playlists. It promised "a dance party in your pocket."
(appears in 8 videos)

**Generic sites get forgotten**
Claim: a site that looks like every other site, especially every other AI-built site, produces no reaction and gets no memory.
Reasoning: "good enough is now free and instant" (his), so looking decent no longer sets anyone apart. The question has become whether a site looks like everyone else's. He calls this "the blanding of the internet" (his). Popular free fonts make the problem worse, because "you're going to look like everybody else" (said by a typography guest on his show).
Example: in a blind test, his hand-designed homepage scored 31.5 out of 40. The best AI tool scored 25.5. One AI page looked "eerily similar" to another, and the judge said it "kind of looks more like a template."
(appears in 8 videos)

**Every visual choice tells visitors who the site is for**
Claim: palette, layout density, type, and imagery should match the brand and the one customer it's built for. The same choices should push away the people it isn't for.
Reasoning: visitors read these signals before they read any words. "If your site doesn't repel someone within five seconds of them landing on a page, the dial isn't turned up enough." (his)
Example: a financial advisor site with a very dark palette, a simple layout, and rock imagery. Without reading anything, he could tell it was built for someone who values order, strength, and seriousness.
(appears in 7 videos)

**"Clean" comes from a system, not from feel**
Claim: consistent spacing and type sizes, set by rules, are what make a site feel clean and organized.
Reasoning: visitors' brains register inconsistency as noise, even when they can't say what's wrong. Their eyes have to bounce unexpectedly from one thing to the next. They also spend most of their screen time on apps like Netflix and Spotify, where every pixel has been tuned, so that is the standard they expect. "Stop designing by feel." (his)
Example: an 8-point spacing system. Gaps inside a group are 16px, gaps between groups are 32px, and the big blocks are evenly spaced at 104px.
(appears in 7 videos)

**Proof beats claims, and specific proof from a source the business doesn't control beats everything**
Claim: visitors want someone other than the business to say it's good, with specifics.
Reasoning: buyers now question everything. They wonder whether a person really said a quote and whether that person is real. He calls this a "trust recession" (his words). A recognizable name, a specific number, or a third-party review platform answers those doubts.
Example: a testimonial from the Chief Marketing Officer of Sprint gave a guest's site "instant credibility." On numbers, "income grew by 21% after we launched their new website" is strong, while "we helped this business grow" is weak.
(appears in 7 videos)

**Every image needs a job**
Claim: images should make people feel something, build trust, or point attention, and they should match the site's colors.
Reasoning: generic stock photos make a site look unintentional. A photo whose colors clash with the palette is "a telltale sign that the person who made the website didn't know what they were doing" (his words). People also look where pictured people are looking.
Example: "Random people smiling at you isn't helping a website. It just makes it look unintentional." (his) His other example of a mismatch is "a neon pinkie on a sleek black and white site."
(appears in 7 videos)

**Get the basics right before adding effects**
Claim: layout, color, type, and images decide whether a site works. Animation and 3D only help once those are solid, and only when they serve the story.
Reasoning: a good animation on a site that hasn't covered the basics is "just tacky" (his words). "Just because you can do it doesn't mean that you should." (his)
Example: "The site has to look good naked." (his) Even his scroll-animation demo site already looked good from spacing and type alone, before any motion was added.
(appears in 7 videos)

**Design for the visitor, not the designer or the owner**
Claim: what matters is whether the people visiting the site respond, not whether the designer or the business owner loves it.
Reasoning: "It doesn't matter if you love the design. It doesn't even matter if your client loves the design." (his) "Fancy designs are mostly made for designers, not users." (his)
Example: his process is to research who actually uses the site, then choose fonts, colors, and layout for that audience instead of personal taste.
(appears in 6 videos)

**Vary the layout down the page, but with a reason**
Claim: changing section structure as you scroll keeps people engaged, while repeating one layout makes them tune out.
Reasoning: brains try to predict how a story ends. When every section looks the same, the visitor assumes they've already seen the ending and stops paying attention. Change for its own sake confuses people, though, so it should follow what the visitor is thinking at that point on the page.
Example: in his blind test, several AI pages stacked one three-column block after another and read as templated. His winning page alternated layouts.
(appears in 6 videos)

**Guide visitors through a story, not a pile of facts**
Claim: the strongest pages lead the visitor through a sequence: their problem, then the solution, then the next step.
Reasoning: "AI can arrange components, but it can't craft an emotional narrative that really guides the user through an entire experience." (his)
Example: the blind-test judge said the AI pages "group these elements together in a couple bullet points," while the human page was "guiding me through a story."
(appears in 6 videos)

**You can't see your own site's problems**
Claim: the person who built the site, or the owner who commissioned it, will miss obvious problems. Only a fresh visitor reveals them.
Reasoning: he calls this the "curse of knowledge" (his phrase). You know where everything is, so you can't see what's confusing. He says it happens on almost every project, even after years of experience.
Example: a first-time visitor "doesn't see that button that you thought was really obvious" (his words).
(appears in 6 videos)

**Use few colors, and give each one a job**
Claim: two or three colors with defined roles beat a big palette. The accent color is kept only for actions.
Reasoning: when only buttons use a certain color, visitors "almost intrinsically know that it's meant for action" (his words). "One good color used well beats five random colors. Any day." (his)
Example: the 60-30-10 rule: about 60% neutrals, 30% brand color, and 10% accent, with the accent going to buttons.
(appears in 6 videos)

**Every screen size needs its own design**
Claim: a desktop layout shrunk onto a phone is not a mobile design.
Reasoning: each screen size has different problems. Four columns get cluttered fast on a phone, and what looks fine in a browser preview can be "a hot mess on an actual iPhone" (his words).
Example: he uses 12 columns on desktop, 8 on tablet, and 4 on mobile, and simplifies layouts a lot on phones.
(appears in 6 videos)

**Speed and broken links quietly cost visitors**
Claim: heavy images, extra plugins, shifting layouts, and dead links frustrate visitors, and Google counts them against the site.
Reasoning: big images are the number one thing that slows sites down. Content that jumps while someone is trying to click is "super annoying" (his words). A link that lands on a 404 page frustrates people and is a bad sign for Google.
Example: a PNG of a few megabytes compared with a WebP of about 100 kilobytes, with little visible difference.
(appears in 6 videos)

**One page, one goal**
Claim: each page should push toward a single action: buy, book a call, or sign up.
Reasoning: "You lose users when you're trying to do too much at once." (his) The goal decides what goes on the page.
Example: a landing page has one purpose and no outbound navigation. "You fly in for a landing, but you have a hard time taking off." (his)
(appears in 5 videos)

**Write about them, not you**
Claim: copy should talk more about the visitor's problem and success than about the business.
Reasoning: buyers want help becoming "the hero" of their own story (his words, drawing on Donald Miller's StoryBrand). Self-focused copy reads as being about the seller's convenience.
Example: his test is that if "I," "me," and "my" outnumber "you" and "your," the copy needs a rewrite.
(appears in 5 videos)

**Keep the structure familiar, and surprise inside it**
Claim: logo, navigation, and footer belong where visitors expect them. Small, pleasant surprises go inside that familiar frame.
Reasoning: visitors arrive with a mental blueprint built from every site they've visited. If something isn't where they expect, "they just see it and say, 'This is different,' and they leave altogether" (his words). Total predictability bores them, though, so he aims for "most advanced yet acceptable" (his words).
Example: the site Gaming Bible moved its mobile menu to the bottom corner, closer to the thumb. Nobody could find it, and they had to move it back.
(appears in 5 videos)

**Every site is a set of trade-offs**
Claim: no site can lead on features, SEO, UX, conversion, speed, accessibility, and design all at once. Someone has to set priorities for each project.
Reasoning: these goals pull against each other. Marketing sometimes runs at odds with accessibility, and SEO sometimes runs at odds with UX.
Example: stripping out every feature and all design would make a site extremely fast, but speed isn't the only thing that matters.
(appears in 3 videos)

## how buyers judge a website

- **Gut reaction comes first, before reading.** Visitors react within seconds with "oh, this is cool" or "I don't like that." A generic site gets no reaction at all, because they've seen it a hundred times.
- **Safety is the first check.** If the logo, menu, or navigation isn't where their mental model expects it, they leave rather than search.
- **Type sets the tone before the words land.** People feel a mismatch immediately, before reading. "Just imagine if your dentist had a logo set in comics" (said by a typography guest).
- **The headline is the fastest test of "am I in the right place."** Burying the location or the type of service, or leading with a clever tagline, leaves visitors unable to decide.
- **Too many services read as no service.** When a business lists many offerings, people come away with "a little bit of this and a little bit of that" and can't describe what it does.
- **Size, contrast, and closeness tell visitors what matters.** Bigger reads as more important. Items placed close together read as connected. Lower-opacity text reads as secondary.
- **Big text blocks make visitors check out.** Bullets, especially bullets with icons, give the eye a place to start.
- **Benefits beyond three or four get forgotten** before the visitor reaches the bottom of the page.
- **Ghost buttons go unseen,** so people don't click them. A visitor can spot the button and still miss the form field next to it if the field blends into the background.
- **Numbers draw the eye.** "People are naturally drawn to numbers on a website" (his words). A numbered list can be understood without reading every word.
- **People follow the gaze of people in photos.** If the pictured person looks away from the headline, visitors may miss it.
- **Mismatched photos signal amateur work.** Photos in different lighting or colors from the rest of the site stand out "like a sore thumb" (his words).
- **A real photo of the owner tells visitors they've arrived at the right business,** especially when they came from that person's channel or profile.
- **Fonts that are almost the same look like a mistake.** Georgia next to Times New Roman reads as unintentional.
- **Layouts that change on every page make a site harder to navigate,** because visitors rely on patterns to find their way around.
- **Total predictability bores visitors.** A small pleasant surprise, like an unusual button hover or an image that scales slightly on scroll, keeps them interested. A big unexpected change scares them off.
- **Slight imperfection reads as human and approachable.** Hand-drawn marks, phone-shot photos, candid video, and bouncy animation signal that a real person made this, which sets it apart from AI-made sites.
- **A sparse page with obvious rules for spacing and type reads as organized and advanced.** A strict grid with no full-width sections reads as stable and serious.
- **Visitors doubt testimonials.** They ask whether the person is real and whether the quote was made up. Voice recordings, phone video, and third-party review sites answer that doubt better than written quotes.
- **A recognizable name and title in a testimonial gives instant credibility.**
- **Content written for keywords is detectable.** "You can tell when content is made for SEO" (said by a guest). Bland stock photos and filler text lose to content with personality.
- **Prices far below the market make buyers suspicious.** A guest compared it to never buying the 50-cent bottle of wine.
- **Copy that sounds flawless makes buyers ask "what's the catch."** If nothing is stated, they imagine something worse than the truth.
- **A creative choice that breaks basic use frustrates people,** for example scrolling that doesn't work the expected way. A site can look beautiful in a portfolio and still fail the person using it.
- **In search results, the page title and description decide the click.** If they don't match the page and give a reason to click, "the click never happens and nothing else really matters" (his words).

## what clients believe and object to

- **"I just want something clean."** Clean means sizes and spacing aren't random. They follow a rhythm and a system, with plenty of white space. A sparse section isn't unfinished.
- **"Just build me a website, and the customers will magically come."** People find a business through Google, then check Instagram, a YouTube video, maybe a Reddit thread, and only then the website. He cites a figure that 82.4% of Gen Z discover new products through influencer marketing, not Google and not a website. The site is one stop on that path and has to be planned that way.
- **"My cousin said they could do it for free" / "Someone else will do it cheaper."** A price objection usually means the value was never made clear. His math: "If you end up getting $10,000 extra every year because I built this website for you, would a $5,000 investment make sense to you?" A cheaper option usually means the owner does more of the work themselves, and "hardly ever is something that's cheaper actually more valuable" (his words).
- **"Why should I pay you when I can just download a template?"** His answer is a question: why not ask your wedding guests to take iPhone photos instead of hiring a photographer? Templates are built generically to reach the widest audience. Someone still has to decide how the design fits this brand and what will turn visitors into customers.
- **"I don't want my website to be cookie cutter" / "I don't want it to look like somebody else's."** That is a real risk, since generic sites get forgotten. A template is only a shell. The colors, fonts, spacing, and content choices are what make it fit the business.
- **"I didn't need to hire somebody, I got AI to build my website."** AI sites tend to look like each other and like the competitor's. In his blind test, the human-designed homepage beat every AI tool by at least 6 points because it guided the visitor through a story instead of grouping bullet points.
- **"I don't need anything fancy, just something simple."** A website's job is outcomes, not features. Cutting the scope too far makes those outcomes less likely.
- **"Just having a website is enough."** "Just having a website doesn't really help your business," and "it doesn't make sense for you to pay me to just build you a pretty website" (both his). The strategy behind the site and how it helps the business grow are what matter.
- **"Do you do SEO?" / "SEO is a button on the back end."** It isn't a button. Most of what a site itself controls comes down to two questions: can Google and people quickly understand what the site is about, and does it load fast and work the way it should?
- **"We have a logo, so our branding is done."** "A brand is much more than a logo" (his words).
- **"I want a modern look."** He reads this as the owner feeling their current site is outdated and being a little embarrassed by it.
- **"I want a wow factor."** Wow-style sites build interaction into nearly every part of the page, and that takes layered skills. It has to fit what the business needs to communicate.
- **"I don't have good photos."** Rounded corners on images and cards plus a warm, natural palette help make up for weak photos. AI can also extend a small set of the client's photos into a full set.
- **"Can you make the logo bigger?"** He treats this as a request to decode. The job is working out what the owner really means.
- **"We're too busy for more business."** The real fear is more work landing on their plate. A site with booking tools and automated follow-up takes work off their plate instead.
- **"I just want to run my own website" / "Can you train me to do this?"** A guest who gets this request says owners who start this way usually give up partway through, because there's a whole stack of things to learn.
- **"My website is done once it launches."** Sites need ongoing updates, security, and speed work. He has had clients return years after launch saying "my site has been hacked and i need your help."
- **"Design won't really affect my business."** His own redesign that looked better and sold worse shows design moves revenue in both directions. So do redesigns that raised revenue 50 to 100 percent.
- **"Can't you just charge us by the hour?"** Hourly billing rewards taking longer. With a fixed price, the owner knows exactly what they'll get and what it will cost, and both sides are focused on the result.

## tactics

### First screen
- Build the hero in this order: headline, then subheader, then one specific button, then an image. The headline comes first because it is the fastest signal that visitors are in the right place.
- Write the headline in words the audience would actually search for, not a clever line. His own was "How to learn web design fast."
- Make the subheader cover the what, the why, and the how of the offer.
- Pair a short brand tagline with one line saying plainly what the business does, and include location and service type for local businesses.
- Put a button in the hero and another in the navigation, then repeat it at intervals down the page.
- Put the best work or best photos at the very top.

### Layout
- Use a 12-column grid on desktop, but most sections only need one to three columns. Stretch content across a few columns so it has room to breathe.
- Keep standard placements: logo top left, navigation next to it, footer at the bottom.
- Alternate image-left and image-right sections, and don't stack identical three-column blocks.
- Hold each section to a handful of ideas. Don't mix features, pricing, and testimonials in one section.
- In bento-box and card grids, make the highest-priority box the brightest or largest, or the eye lands on the wrong one.
- Group footers and five-plus column areas into clear clusters, or they become "visual noise that people just glance over" (his words).
- Reuse the homepage's sections and patterns on the other pages.
- Use a before-and-after layout with icons for bad versus good, grayed-out "before" text, and darker "after" text.
- When content updates often, as with blog posts, check the layout both completely full and completely empty.

### Copy
- Keep lines to 50 to 75 characters (about 600 to 700px wide) and add a line break every two or three sentences.
- Turn dense paragraphs into bullets, and add icons to the bullets.
- Group benefits into three or four chunks instead of one long list.
- On pricing tiers, show only the difference ("everything in Basic, plus...") instead of repeating every feature under every tier.
- Cut any text that repeats what the header already says.
- Use one H1 per page that plainly says what the page is about, "not clever or cute" (his words). Use H2s for sections and H3s for supporting points.
- Count "I/me/my" against "you/your" and rewrite if the business wins.
- Use the customer's own words and plain language, not industry jargon.
- Write the content first in a document, then place it in the design.
- Put a reassurance where the eye will land, for example "No obligation. You can reserve a car for a short time and pause it whenever you like."

### Typography
- Use one or two typefaces, three at most. A super family (fonts designed together, like DM Sans and DM Serif) is a safe pairing.
- If you pair fonts, make them clearly different but from the same world, such as a condensed serif headline with a wide sans-serif body.
- Save display or decorative fonts for short, large text. Anything longer than about 7 to 10 words should be easy to read.
- Base body text at 16px and scale headings up by a set ratio (about 25% per step).
- Set paragraph line height to about 1.5 times the font size, and headline line height to about 1 times.
- Tighten letter spacing on big headlines (-1 to -3px), leave body text at default, and space out buttons and all-caps text slightly (+1 to +3px).
- Use text opacity for hierarchy: headline at 100%, subheading around 70%.
- Avoid the most overused free fonts if the brand needs to stand out.

### Color
- Follow 60-30-10: about 60% neutrals, 30% brand color, and 10% accent, with the accent used only on buttons.
- Check contrast: at least 4.5 to 1 for small text and 3 to 1 for large text.
- Make color variations from opacity of the main color instead of adding new colors.
- Don't use color as the only signal. Pair success and error states with icons or labels.
- Use loud neon sparingly. People tire of it quickly.

### Imagery
- Choose photos that look like they came from the same shoot, with good lighting, natural expressions, and colors that match the palette.
- Pick photos where people look toward the headline or button.
- Use a real photo of the owner instead of stock when the business is personal.
- On images that carry text, darken the background, lower the image opacity, and shorten the copy.
- Crop with the subject on the left or right third of the frame.
- Add a soft drop shadow to an image that blends into the background.

### Proof
- Prefer voice recordings or phone video over written testimonials, and prefer Google Reviews or other third-party platforms over quotes the business hosts itself.
- Use specific numbers ("income grew by 21%") rather than general praise.
- Spread testimonials throughout the site, worded around goals the customer reached.
- Show the work and a peek behind the scenes, especially for visual businesses like landscapers.
- Use case studies built as problem, goal, and how it was solved.

### Conversion
- Give each page one goal, and map the path backward from the thank-you or confirmation page to the entry point. Fix any dead end or point of hesitation along the way.
- Use solid, high-contrast buttons with action labels ("See pricing," "Download the guide"), never ghost buttons.
- Give form fields a visible background so they don't blend in.
- Test one change at a time: headline style, button wording, image placement.
- Set up Google Analytics with key events (purchase, form fill, booked call) and watch whether that number moves after a change.

### Motion and polish
- Get the page looking good without motion first, then add effects.
- Pick one standout element (a font, an image, an object) that ties to the business's story, and keep everything else supporting it.
- Repeat small details from that element, or from the logo, in buttons, icons, and cards ("visual rhyming," his phrase).
- Keep texture and depth subtle: light grain, a glass effect on cards, text tucked slightly behind an image.
- Add sounds only if they're tiny, like a soft click, and let visitors turn them on themselves.

### Mobile
- Design mobile at the same time as desktop, not afterward.
- Use one or two columns on phones. Use swipeable rows when stacking would make visitors scroll too much.
- Make small tap targets like arrows bigger.
- Test on real phones, not just browser previews.

### Speed and search
- Export images at the exact size needed, as WebP, around 300KB or less.
- Skip plugins and third-party tools the site doesn't truly need.
- Write descriptive alt text for every image.
- Add a redirect whenever a page is deleted or a URL changes.
- Give every page its own title and description.
- Make leaving a Google review one click by sending customers the direct review link.

### Testing
- Show the page to someone for a few seconds and ask what they remember, in order. If the most important thing is missing, fix the hierarchy.
- Squint at the page or blur a screenshot. The headline and button should still stand out.
- Have someone unconnected to the business think out loud while scrolling, then give them a real task to complete.
- Ask testers: does the headline make sense immediately, is it clear what the site is about, do you know what to do next, does it work on your phone?
- Before launch, submit every form, place a test order, and sign up for every freebie to confirm the emails arrive.
- Scroll the finished page from bottom to top to see it fresh.
