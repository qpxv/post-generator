# X post performance report: Ben Winzer

## 1. Verdict

There is a small learnable signal and a lot of noise. Of seven tested features across roughly 1,385 posts, only two clear p < 0.05: short posts underperform, and evening posts underperform. Both effects are modest, around 7 to 10 percentile points at the median. That does not justify a weekly prompt-insight loop. A week holds about 20 posts (roughly 12 website, 8 personal), so any "insight" drawn from one week will mostly be chasing luck, and feeding that back into the prompt will make the pipeline drift randomly. Keep the daily stats collection, since it is cheap and it built this dataset. Replace weekly insights with a quarterly review that tests a few hypotheses written down in advance. Also add a metric closer to the actual goal (profile visits, DMs, replies from coaches) because views alone are a weak proxy for clients.

## 2. What the numbers say

**Month table: reach is driven by the account, not the posts.**

- Median views swing about 5x between eras: 146 to 195 in Aug to Sep 2025, a long slide down to 35 to 54 from Dec 2025 through Jun 2026, then a rebound to 97 to 148 in Jul to Sep 2026. No single post feature explains swings that size.
- "Posts with an outside reply" fell from 93 to 97% in Aug to Sep 2025 to 33 to 58% in 2026. The early peak looks like reply activity (Ben replying a lot, or reply circles) feeding distribution. The decline in outside replies tracks the decline in median views through mid 2026.
- The Jul to Sep 2026 rebound is not explained by replies, which stayed at 38 to 49%. Something else changed (algorithm, subscription, a follower bump, posting pattern). The data cannot say what.
- In Jul to Sep 2026 the top posts cluster tightly at 983 to 1,238 views but have only 2 to 18 likes. Example: the barber post got 983 views and 2 likes. These are impression bursts from feed distribution, not posts that people engaged with heavily. Top percentile here means "got pushed," not necessarily "resonated."
- Nov 2024 (34 posts) and Dec 2024 (4 posts) are too small to rank inside. A pct 100 post in a 4-post month carries almost no information. Exclude 2024 from any future analysis.

**Feature table.**

One complication with "short": the 2025 one-liners ("Your excuses sound dumber when you say them out loud.", 2,999 views) top their months, while 2026 short posts sit at the bottom. The effect may be era-dependent rather than universal. The pooled result says short loses. The 2026 examples below say that more strongly.

## 3. Website posts

First, a warning about the evidence. Each month has about 50 to 55 website posts, and the top and bottom extremes are where luck concentrates. The clearest sign of noise: the same thesis appears at both ends.

- "the coach with the worse offer but the better website wins" (2026-04, pct 0, 11 views)
- "a business can have great reviews ... and still LOSE the client to someone with a worse offer" (2026-06, pct 100)

Treat everything below as patterns to test, not proven rules.

**Patterns in the top posts**

1. **They open with something the buyer says or experiences, not a rule.**
   - "before you say 'i can't afford a professional website'"
   - "i hear this all the time: 'i'm just testing the market first'"
   - "you never get the email that says..."

   Compare these bottom openers, which are instructions:
   - "Your website should load the most important content first." (pct 1)
   - "Your website should answer the following question in 5 seconds" (pct 1)
   - "Please stop using stock photos on your website." (pct 0)
   - "Social proof on a website works only if it's specific." (pct 3)

2. **They list concrete page elements a stranger sees.** The Jul to Sep 2026 winners almost all list the same specifics:
   - "a stock photo of two people shaking hands in an office"
   - "we help businesses grow"
   - "the testimonials if they exist have no last name"
   - "there is no face on the page"
   - "nothing on the page proves that anyone has ever paid for this"

   The pct 0 April post makes the same argument with no element named: "they're going to click the one that looks the highest quality."

3. **The real-person story has dialogue or a number.**
   - The certifications post: "do your clients know what any of this means", "14 fucking seconds", "2 minutes 15 seconds".
   - The pricing story: "i said 1 grand / nothing more / just the number".

   The bottom 34,000-coaches post has stats, but no single interaction carries it.

4. **Worked math lands.** Both 2026-01 math posts are top ("revenue per visitor: $6,000 ÷ 500 = $12", "$150/hr × 25 hours = $3,750"). The bottom ads post asserts the same logic without showing the work: "coaches will spend 2k a month on ads but refuse to spend 2k on the website".

5. **Winning analogies map onto a buyer or owner behavior.**
   - Barber: "i own scissors ... every person who looked at me would have known within ONE second", which maps onto DIY sites, then names specific defects ("the hero photo got stretched wide").
   - Roommate: "completely unbothered" maps onto the owner who "know[s] how good they are".
   - Cold coffee: "they showed up because they needed something".

**Patterns in the bottom posts**

1. **Animal and nature analogies.** Four bottom website posts lean on birds or animals: the parrot (pct 0), the campsite birds (pct 1), the kid launching bread (pct 1), and the magpies plus popup (pct 3). No top website post uses one. They also map onto abstract principles ("feel DIFFERENT", "feel safe to land on", "flow") rather than a buyer action. The einaudi (pct 1) and mountain biker (pct 2) posts share this: a vivid moment, then an abstract design virtue.
2. **Very short aphorisms.**
   - "a coach with a bad website is invisible / not because nobody finds them / because nobody STAYS" (pct 1)
   - The hotel shower (pct 1)
   - "you don't know which day someone lands on your website ready to pay you" (pct 3)

   This lines up with the one real feature.
3. **Long structured frameworks with no person in them.** The content hub post, the executive vs relationship coach post, and the eye-direction post are all pct 2. They read like blog sections.
4. **A website post with no point.** The grooming service post ("Literally just because I love animals", 3 views).

One oddity: "full website design for a design app landing page" is pct 97 with one line of text. It is almost certainly a media post. The feature table has no media flag, so this is invisible to the analysis.

## 4. Personal posts

**Top**

1. **A stance aimed at "you," usually about money, work or discipline.** In 2025 these were one-liners:
   - "Your $0 income does not need a fucking morning routine."
   - "You aren't a bear, you are just lazy as fuck."

   In 2026 they are longer but still a stance: "i don't know how people do 9-5 for 40 years / genuinely cannot compute it".
2. **A specific moment that ends in a usable takeaway.**
   - Food: "one portion chicken and rice / two brötchen with salami" ending in "it's a system thing".
   - Mirror: "just leaned it up against the wall".
   - The hour-long task: "you can't move fast when half your brain is narrating how slow you are".
3. **Opinionated reactions to something concrete.** Claude vs ChatGPT ("BROTHER"), the kid screaming at his mother, grayscale mode.

**Bottom**

1. **Nature observation with no point.**
   - Magpies: "absolutely beautiful", pct 0.
   - Squirrel: "genuinely more entertaining than most things on my phone", pct 0.
   - "sometimes i think animals understand life better than us", pct 0.
   - Dogs being dogs, pct 1.

   This is the most consistent pattern in the whole dataset, and it repeats the website finding: animal content sits at the bottom in both categories.
2. **Newsletter promos with a link.** Both "Tonight's email ... Link ↓" posts are bottom. X is widely reported to downrank posts with external links. That is outside knowledge, not something this data tests.
3. **Generic motivation.**
   - "Pick your hard." (pct 0)
   - "They're just braver." (pct 0)

   Generic 2024 lines also appear at the top ("You'll get ghosted"). The 2024 months are too small to read either way.
4. **Lectures about other people.** The sunset rant ("people who cant even watch a sunset anymore are so fucked") and exam anxiety ("how can you be afraid of a piece of paper??") both scold rather than show something about Ben.
5. **An empty post and a meme caption** ("Business coaches be like"), which are probably media or broken posts.

Note that several "personal" posts are actually business advice ("Starting from $XYZ", the coaches offer post, the specificity post). The category label is leaky, which blurs both comparisons.

## 5. What to change in the generation prompt

These are hypotheses with weak to moderate support. Only the first rests on a statistically real feature. Run them for a quarter and check again, rather than treating them as settled.

1. **Do not produce short posts.** Every post needs a build-up of at least several lines before the point lands. No two-to-four-line aphorisms, either website or personal. *Evidence: "short" p 0.000, plus the 2026 bottom examples above.*
2. **Website posts: open with the buyer's words, objection or situation, not a "your website should" rule.** *Evidence: top openers quote the reader ("i'm just testing the market first"). Bottom openers are instructions.*
3. **Website posts: land the point on two or more named page elements a stranger would see** (the stock photo, the hero line, testimonials with initials, no face, no client names) or on worked numbers. Do not end on a general virtue like "quality", "flow" or "different". *Evidence: the Jul to Sep 2026 winners and both 2026-01 math posts, versus the pct 0 April post making the same argument without specifics.*
4. **When a journal moment becomes an analogy, it must map onto a specific owner or buyer behavior** (cutting your own hair = DIY site, unbothered roommate = blind owner). Do not use animal or nature moments as website analogies, and do not turn nature-only journal entries into personal posts unless they carry a stance or takeaway about work. *Evidence: animal posts sit at the bottom in both categories, and no animal post appears in either top list. This pattern has not been statistically tested, so add an `animalOrNature` feature and test it.*
5. **No external links or "Link ↓" promos in the post body.** *Evidence: both link posts are bottom. This is a sample of 2 plus the known platform behavior, so it is the weakest of the five.*

Outside the prompt: shift posting away from the evening slot if that is easy to do (p 0.010, borderline).

## 6. Caveats

- **Account-level drift dominates.** Reply activity, algorithm changes and whatever caused the Jul 2026 rebound move reach far more than any post feature. Within-month percentiles cancel the level shift, but not changes in *which kind* of post gets pushed in a given era. Short one-liners won in 2025 and lose in 2026.
- **Topic of the month.** Jul to Sep 2026 website posts converge on one template (stock photo, "we help businesses grow", no proof). Within-month ranking then compares that template mostly against itself and against analogy posts, so the "named elements win" finding is partly "the dominant template that month won."
- **The pipeline changed.** 2025 posts are sentence case with heavy caps. 2026 posts are lowercase and longer. Style, length and era are tangled together, and the "short" effect may partly be an era artifact.
- **Selection of extremes.** Top and bottom lists out of ~90 posts a month are where randomness concentrates. Expect regression to the mean if you copy them.
- **Views are not the goal.** Posts at 1,000+ views with 2 likes reached feeds, not buyers. None of this measures whether a coach DMed.
- **Missing features.** Media vs text-only, link presence, animal or nature content, and reply activity on the day are not in the table, and at least one top post is clearly a media post.
- **Multiple comparisons.** Seven tests at p < 0.05 means roughly a one-in-three chance that at least one "real" result is a false positive. The short effect is robust. The evening effect is borderline.
